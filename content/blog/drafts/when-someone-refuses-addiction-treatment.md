It is incredibly difficult to watch a family member struggle with substance use, especially when they refuse professional help. This post covers practical, non-confrontational ways to approach your loved one, how to shift your communication style to reduce defensiveness, and where to find support for your own well-being while navigating this journey.

## Shifting away from confrontation

When a loved one rejects the idea of detox or rehab, our instinct is often to argue, plead, or present logical evidence of the harm their addiction is causing. Unfortunately, confrontation usually leads to defensiveness, denial, and further isolation. Shifting to a non-confrontational approach does not mean you agree with their choices. Instead, it means you prioritize keeping the lines of communication open.

Try to avoid lecturing or issuing ultimatums when emotions are running high. Instead, focus on expressing your own feelings using "I" statements rather than "you" statements. For example, saying "I feel scared when I do not hear from you" is often received better than "You are ruining this family with your drinking."

## Finding the right moments to talk

Timing is crucial when discussing treatment options. Attempting to have a serious conversation when your loved one is actively under the influence or experiencing withdrawal is rarely productive. Look for quiet, calm moments when they are sober and relatively relaxed.

When you do speak, focus on listening more than talking. Ask open-ended questions about how they are feeling and what they want for their future. Sometimes, simply feeling heard and understood can make a person more open to considering change.

## Offering low-pressure options

Often, the idea of entering a long-term residential treatment program feels overwhelming or terrifying to someone struggling with addiction. If they refuse inpatient rehab, consider suggesting smaller, less intimidating steps. This might include talking to a family doctor, attending a single counseling session, or joining a mutual support group.

It is also helpful to gather information quietly so you are prepared if they do express a moment of readiness. For those in Ontario, [ConnexOntario](https://connexontario.ca) provides free, confidential health services information for people experiencing problems with alcohol and drugs. Having these resources on hand allows you to offer immediate, concrete options without putting pressure on your loved one before they are ready.

## Taking care of yourself

We cannot force another person to change, but we can control how we take care of ourselves. Supporting someone with an addiction takes a heavy emotional and physical toll. Establishing clear boundaries is essential for your own mental health.

Seeking support for yourself is just as important as finding help for your loved one. You do not have to carry this burden alone, and connecting with professionals who understand the complexities of addiction can provide you with much-needed guidance. If you are in the United States, you can reach out to the [SAMHSA National Helpline](https://www.samhsa.gov/find-help/national-helpline) for free, confidential treatment referral and information.
