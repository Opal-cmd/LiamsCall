When someone you love will not accept help, lectures rarely open the door. This guide offers a calmer approach: one small conversation, one clear ask, and room for a no without abandoning safety.

Start by naming what you see without diagnosing. “I’ve noticed you haven’t slept in days and I’m worried” lands better than a label. [NAMI’s family caregiver pages](https://www.nami.org/Your-Journey/Family-Members-and-Caregivers) emphasize education and support for families walking this line.

Ask for one concrete next step, not a full treatment plan. Examples: “Will you sit with me while I call a helpline?” or “Can we look at ConnexOntario together for ten minutes?” In Ontario, [ConnexOntario](https://connexontario.ca) matches people to current mental health and addiction services. In the U.S., [SAMHSA’s National Helpline](https://www.samhsa.gov/find-help/national-helpline) is a starting directory.

If they say no, do not stack five more arguments in the same hour. Protect your own sleep and one boundary. If safety collapses — overdose, violence, suicidal crisis — switch to [9-1-1](tel:911) or [9-8-8](tel:988). Refusal of voluntary help does not mean you must face danger alone.

Write down what you tried. It helps the next professional conversation and reduces the sense that every talk starts from zero.
